Above the Cloud Line: Cloud Hill Capital Founding Manifesto

Introduction

The Future of Humanity

For 13.8 billion years the universe has been learning to process information on ever faster substrates. We are living through the next change of substrate.

Mountaineers have an old observation: from high enough ground, you can see the weather before it reaches the valley. The storm is already visible on the horizon, its direction and speed legible, while the people below are still planning their afternoon. That is the vantage this firm is named for, and the vantage this essay asks you to share for a moment. From up here, the shape of the next decade is unusually clear. Down in the valley, most of the world has not yet looked up.

Start further back than economics. Start with the universe.

For roughly the first nine billion years after the Big Bang, the cosmos did one thing well: it made matter more complicated. Hydrogen fused into helium; stars forged carbon, oxygen and iron in their cores; supernovae scattered the heavy elements across space. Then, around four billion years ago, on one unremarkable rock, chemistry learned to copy itself. Information acquired a substrate: the molecule. For the next three billion years, natural selection wrote and rewrote that molecular code, slowly, one generation at a time.

Then the substrate changed. Nervous systems allowed organisms to model their environment within a single lifetime rather than across generations. Some three hundred thousand years ago a primate appeared that could store information outside its genome entirely, in language, and later in writing, mathematics and institutions. Culture became the new substrate, and it ran orders of magnitude faster than genetics. Agriculture arrived twelve thousand years ago. Writing, five thousand. The printing press, under six hundred. The steam engine, under three hundred. The transistor, eighty.

Carl Sagan once compressed the history of the universe into a single calendar year. On that calendar, all of written history occupies the final eleven seconds of 31 December. The industrial revolution occupies the last half second. Each transition arrives faster than the one before, and each one moves cognition onto a substrate that is faster, cheaper and more scalable than its predecessor: from molecules, to neurons, to culture, to silicon.

What we are now living through will not register on that calendar as another second. It will register as the moment the calendar changed hands.

Superintelligence is the next change of substrate. For the entirety of human history, the number of minds working on any problem has been bounded by the number of humans who could be born, educated and paid. That constraint is about to dissolve. When cognition can be instantiated in silicon, copied at the marginal cost of electricity and run at many times human speed, the supply of intelligence ceases to be a demographic quantity and becomes an industrial one. It will be manufactured, metered and allocated like steel or electricity. This is the most consequential economic fact of our lifetimes, and very few balance sheets have absorbed it.

The world, phase by phase

The trendlines have been remarkably stable. Effective compute devoted to frontier training has grown by roughly half an order of magnitude per year, with algorithmic efficiency compounding on top. Our timeline borrows its skeleton from Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness, and we find its logic difficult to escape. The world passes through three phases. First, the economy bends around building AI. Then it is rebuilt by AI. Finally, discovery itself is industrialised, as parallel, compounding research operates at a scale no civilisation has ever commanded.

2025–2027: the buildout. The economy reorganises around the construction of compute. The four largest hyperscalers alone have guided to capital expenditure on the order of seven hundred billion dollars for 2026, roughly double the prior year, and the full industrial mobilisation behind them runs well beyond that. Electricity becomes the strategic commodity of the age. Large power transformers, gas turbines and grid interconnections carry multi-year waiting lists. Energy, utilities, industrials and semiconductors lead. For most households, the transition is felt only as inflation in power, construction labour and skilled trades. AI revenue compounds quickly but trails capex, and so markets oscillate between declaring a bubble and declaring the opportunity underpriced. Both camps are watching the wrong variable: the question is not whether today's revenue justifies today's spend, but whether the capability curve justifies tomorrow's.

2027–2029: AGI and automated research. Systems operating at roughly expert human level run as millions of parallel instances, around the clock, without fatigue or turnover. The first domain to be fully automated is the one that matters most: AI research itself. This closes the feedback loop at the centre of the thesis. Remote cognitive work is automated at scale: software engineering, analysis, legal drafting, research, and much of finance and consulting. Productivity statistics move for the first time in a generation. White-collar labour markets come under acute stress while physical trades remain tight. States respond as states always have when a technology becomes decisive: export controls tighten, laboratories fall under security regimes, and the frontier is drawn into something resembling a national project.

2029 onward: superintelligence. Intelligence ceases to be the binding constraint. What remains scarce is everything that cannot be reasoned into existence: energy, materials, physical labour, land, regulatory approval and political legitimacy. Growth could break from the two to three per cent per year that has defined the industrial era toward rates without historical precedent, because the ancient ceiling on growth, the number of human researchers and workers, has been removed.

The economics of abundant cognition

The second-order effects are as important as the first. Returns to capital rise against returns to cognitive labour. Owning compute and energy becomes structurally more valuable than selling expertise by the hour, and inequality becomes the central political question of the decade, with redistribution, compute taxation and income-floor policies moving from the margins to the mainstream.

Deflation and inflation arrive together. Anything made of thought trends toward free: software, analysis, design, professional services. Anything made of atoms that AI demand competes for becomes dearer: power, chips, land near substations, copper. The economy splits along a single seam, and the seam runs between bits and atoms.

Real rates rise. When capital becomes enormously productive, its price climbs. That pressure falls hardest on long-duration assets whose future cash flows the new economy is busy disrupting. Geopolitics becomes economics. Markets bifurcate along a US-allied and Chinese axis; chip embargoes, capital controls and security clearances determine who may participate. Firms change shape. Companies operate with extraordinarily few people relative to output, and the binding constraint migrates from talent to compute allocation and permission to deploy.

How breakthroughs happen

The mechanism is not a single genius machine. It is research conducted at a scale and velocity humanity has never possessed. Parallelism: millions of researcher-equivalents work simultaneously, each at many times human speed, compressing a decade of a field's progress into months. Self-improvement: AI improves AI first; each algorithmic gain accelerates the next round. This is the intelligence explosion, and it is less science fiction than compound interest applied to cognition. Exhaustive search: rather than testing a handful of hypotheses, systems explore entire design spaces of molecules, materials, proofs and chip architectures. Closing the lab loop: robotics and automated laboratories let AI run its own experiments, removing the slowest human step in physical science.

The first breakthroughs will likely arrive in drug discovery and biology, materials and batteries, fission and fusion engineering, chip design, mathematics and robotics. Robotics is decisive among them, because it is the bridge by which superintelligence crosses from the digital economy into the physical one.

Where we part from the canonical account

We are not naive about friction. Fabs, power plants and permits still take years, however brilliant the planning. Diffusion is slow: Paul David showed that electricity took four decades to appear in American productivity data, because factories had to be redesigned around the dynamo before its gains could be realised. Mass displacement of white-collar work could provoke a political backlash that slows deployment. And above all of these sits alignment: if control fails, none of the upside matters. Friction does not falsify the thesis. It determines where value accrues, and it is precisely where we intend to invest.

Our mission

Cloud Hill Capital is a venture capital firm investing in growth-stage companies across every layer of the intelligence economy: energy, compute and infrastructure, inference, safety and security, applied intelligence and defence. Our mission is to own and strengthen the companies building the physical and institutional foundations of superintelligence, and to direct that capital toward a transition that remains abundant, secure and under human control.

I. What Intelligence Cannot Make →

Chapter I

What Intelligence Cannot Make

The winners of the intelligence economy will be the companies that own whatever superintelligence cannot produce for itself quickly: physical capacity, state trust and control over who reaches the frontier.

In 1849, roughly three hundred thousand people descended on California in search of gold. Most of them left poorer than they arrived. The fortunes that endured belonged to those who controlled what every prospector needed regardless of whether he struck a seam: Levi Strauss sold trousers, Samuel Brannan sold shovels, and the merchants of San Francisco sold flour at prices that would embarrass a modern monopolist. The lesson is usually told as a parable about picks and shovels. Its deeper version is about scarcity. In any rush, value flows to whatever remains scarce when everything else becomes abundant.

The intelligence economy is the largest rush in history, and its central paradox is the same. The output being produced, cognition, is about to become the least scarce input in the economy. Its marginal cost will fall toward the price of the electricity required to run it. The question for an investor is therefore not who will produce intelligence, but what intelligence will still need, and cannot conjure, once it is abundant.

The physics of the bottleneck

A superintelligent system can design a better reactor in an afternoon. It cannot pour the concrete, forge the pressure vessel or obtain the licence in an afternoon. It can optimise a chip layout beyond the reach of any human team, but the extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines that print it are built by a single supplier and shipped in dozens of containers. It can plan a gigawatt campus to the last cable tray, but the transformers on its single-line diagram carry delivery times measured in years, and the interconnection queue ahead of it is longer still.

This is the structure of the next decade. The binding constraints are not cognitive. They are power, chips, fabrication capacity, transformers, cooling, land and permits. Each of these is governed by physics, by industrial lead times or by the slow machinery of the state, and none of them yields to cleverness alone. When every participant in the economy is competing for the same scarce atoms, the holder of those atoms sets the price.

The profile of the winning cohort

Owners of physical bottlenecks. This is the most direct expression of the thesis: leading-edge fabs and the equipment makers behind them, GPU and networking suppliers, grid equipment and generation, and above all firm power. Gas, nuclear and any technology that can be online before the end of the decade will command a premium no software margin can match. Datacenter developers that already control energised sites belong here too. A company such as Crusoe, which built its model around locating compute where stranded or abundant energy already exists, is the archetype: it treats the electron, not the GPU, as the scarce input.

Frontier laboratories, with an important caveat. The two or three laboratories at the frontier will capture extraordinary value during the run-up. Yet the logic of national security points toward a future in which governments draw the frontier into their orbit through forced partnerships, capped returns or something approaching nationalisation. A position in a frontier lab is a position in the period before the state arrives, plus whatever terms holders receive once it does. We underwrite that honestly, and we favour laboratories whose posture on security and control makes them the partners a state would choose rather than the ones it would constrain.

National security integrators. If the race to general intelligence becomes a security contest, and every signal suggests it will, value flows to firms already trusted inside the defence establishment: defence technology primes in the mould of Anduril, cleared cloud and secure compute providers, and companies that protect model weights and laboratory infrastructure. Clearances, accreditation and procurement relationships accumulate over years and cannot be purchased in a funding round. That is precisely what makes them durable.

Holders of non-replicable assets. These are businesses whose advantage was never cognitive labour in the first place: proprietary physical-world data, regulated licences, physical distribution networks, and hard assets such as mines, materials and land near power. When intelligence is cheap, anything that cannot be reasoned into existence is repriced upward. A licence to operate, a proprietary dataset of real-world measurements or a mineral deposit will matter more in 2032 than in 2022, because the cognitive arbitrage that used to compete with them will have collapsed.

When intelligence is abundant, the market stops paying for thinking and starts paying for permission, proximity and physics.

Who is exposed

The mirror image is equally instructive. Thin application layers that wrap a frontier model in an interface, without proprietary data, distribution or compute of their own, will find their margins absorbed by the models beneath them. Each generation of capability erases a category of wrapper. Inference businesses face the same pressure unless they control the compute and power beneath them. Labour-arbitrage services in consulting, outsourcing and much of financial and legal work lose the basis of their pricing, because the scarce expert hour that justified it is no longer scarce. Companies outside the allied bloc face a structural discount if the division between the free world and its rivals hardens into durable export and capital controls.

None of this implies that software ceases to matter. It implies that software without a defensible claim on something scarce will be priced as a commodity. The question we ask of every application-layer company is simple: when the next model arrives, what do you own that it cannot replicate?

The common traits

Across every category, the winners share four characteristics. They are capital-intensive, which deters casual competition. They are physically constrained, which converts time into a moat. They are government-aligned and domiciled in allied jurisdictions, which secures their right to participate as the market fractures. And they are scarce in a way that intelligence alone cannot fix, which is the property that ultimately sets their value.

These traits also explain why the opportunity sits at the growth stage. A seed-stage company has an idea about the bottleneck; a growth-stage company is beginning to own it. It has the site, the queue position, the licence, the first offtake contract or the first accredited deployment. Its technical risk is largely retired, while the full value of its scarcity has not yet been priced. That window, between the proof and the scale, is where Cloud Hill Capital operates.

Where the thesis is weakest

If the frontier is ultimately absorbed into a state-led programme, the largest single prize may accrue to governments rather than shareholders. The most resilient exposure is therefore the foundational layer the state will need and pay for, together with the safety and security capabilities it will insist upon, not only the frontier itself. We build the portfolio accordingly.

II. The Creation of a Private Economy →

Chapter II

The Creation of a Private Economy

The intelligence economy consumes capital at a scale public markets were never designed to supply, and its most important companies no longer need them.

If the first chapter holds, the next question is practical: where does the capital go, and who gets to own what it builds?

The answer begins with an observation about scale. Training frontier systems, building the campuses that house them and securing the power that keeps them running now require sums that would once have financed entire industries. The companies doing this work have little reason to list their shares. Private capital has become deep enough, patient enough and strategic enough to fund them through every stage of their development, and remaining private protects the three things they value most: speed, secrecy and control.

Consider the historical function of an initial public offering. For most of the twentieth century, listing was how a company accessed capital at scale. The public market was the only reservoir large enough to finance a railroad, a steel mill or a national telephone network. That is no longer true. Sovereign wealth funds, strategic corporates, crossover funds and the largest venture platforms now write cheques that exceed most IPOs, without the quarterly disclosure, short-term pressure or regulatory surface that a listing entails. The reservoir has moved upstream.

The first quarter of 2026 was defined by four record-scale financings: OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI and Waymo. OpenAI's $122 billion raise at an $852 billion valuation is the largest venture round ever recorded. Rounds of that magnitude once marked a company's arrival on a public exchange. Today they are simply the next private round.

The collapse of the funding timeline

The traditional sequence of seed, Series A, growth and IPO, each stage separated by proof points and years of execution, has been compressed almost beyond recognition. What once took a decade now takes quarters.

Instinct, a personal AI agent still operating on an invitation basis, raised $1 billion at a $10 billion valuation in September, four times the $2.5 billion mark set in its Series B roughly a month earlier. It has disclosed no revenue. Its investors are not underwriting present cash flows. They are purchasing a position in a market they expect to exist at enormous scale within a few years, and they are paying for the option rather than the asset.

The seed stage, once the cheapest point of entry, has changed most of all. In April, David Silver's Ineffable Intelligence raised a $1.1 billion seed round at a $5.1 billion valuation, led by Sequoia and Lightspeed, with Nvidia, Google, the UK's Sovereign AI Fund and the British Business Bank participating. The company was formed in November 2025. It followed Yann LeCun's AMI Labs, which raised $1.03 billion in March. The median US seed round, by contrast, is roughly $4 million. The distance between the typical seed and the frontier seed now exceeds two orders of magnitude. These are not the same asset with different price tags. They are different species.

Isomorphic Labs shows the same pattern in science. After a $600 million first external round led by Thrive Capital in 2025, it raised a $2.1 billion Series B in May 2026, again led by Thrive, with Alphabet and GV returning and Temasek, MGX, CapitalG and the UK Sovereign AI Fund joining. A drug discovery company has become an infrastructure-scale capital project, because the instrument of discovery is now a compute cluster.

The lesson is structural, not cyclical. When the cost of competing at the frontier is denominated in compute, rounds must be sized for compute. And when the eventual prize is denominated in trillions, rational investors will pay extraordinary entry prices to secure a seat. Critics call this a bubble. It may contain bubbles. But a market that prices options on a transformation of the global economy is not irrational merely because the strike prices are large.

Energy as currency

In this economy, capital alone is insufficient. A billion dollars that cannot be converted into energised compute is of limited use to a frontier laboratory. The real unit of account is becoming the megawatt.

Capital is already moving accordingly. Of the ten largest venture rounds in August 2026, seven went to companies building physical things: reactors, batteries, satellites, missiles, robots and the factories that make them. Valar Atomics raised $1 billion for small modular reactors designed to power compute; Form Energy raised $750 million for multi-day grid storage.

For most of the twentieth century, the world's reserve asset was backed, explicitly or implicitly, by gold. Gold earned that role because it was scarce, universally accepted and impossible to print. Energy now has the same properties within the intelligence economy. It is scarce, it is required for every transaction the new economy performs, and no central bank can create it. A company holding a secured, interconnected gigawatt holds something no rival can replicate quickly, however much capital that rival raises. Power purchase agreements, grid queue positions and permitted land are becoming the reserve assets of the age. We are moving, in effect, from a gold standard to a watt standard.

How wealth will be created

Prosperity in this economy will be immense, and it will be distributed unevenly by construction. It accrues in three layers.

The first layer belongs to whoever owns the inputs: energy, compute, fabrication and the physical supply chain. These are the toll roads of the new economy, and every unit of intelligence produced pays to travel them.

The second layer belongs to the frontier itself: the laboratories and the applied companies that convert intelligence into products such as drugs, materials, agents and autonomous systems. Value creation here will be extreme, and nearly all of it will occur before any public listing. By the time these companies reach the public markets, if they ever do, most of the compounding will already have happened in private hands.

The third layer belongs to society at large, through the collapsing cost of everything intelligence can produce. Medicine, software, education and professional services become dramatically cheaper. That is genuine prosperity, but it arrives as lower prices and better outcomes rather than as ownership.

The gap between those who own the first two layers and those who only benefit from the third will define the economic politics of the coming decade.

For investors, the implication is direct. Exposure to this economy means owning its companies privately, and owning them while their value is still being created rather than after it has been realised. Public markets will eventually reflect this transition, but they will reflect it late, through a narrowing set of listed proxies, at prices that already discount most of the gains. The compounding is happening upstream, at the growth stage, in rounds most institutions never see.

That raises the question the next chapter addresses: if the most important companies of the coming decade are private, what kind of instrument allows an investor to own them?

III. The Emergence of a New Asset Class →

Chapter III

The Emergence of a New Asset Class

Every great technological shift mints its own instrument of ownership. The intelligence economy is minting one now.

Technological revolutions do not only create new industries. They create new ways of owning industries, because the capital requirements of each revolution outgrow the financial architecture of the one before it.

The railways of the nineteenth century required more capital than any family fortune or merchant bank could supply, and so they gave rise to the modern corporate bond market and much of the apparatus of the London and New York exchanges. Industrialisation, with its steel, oil and automobiles, produced public equity as we understand it: widely held, continuously priced, professionally analysed. The post-war expansion of pension savings created the institutional asset manager. And the semiconductor and internet eras produced venture capital as an institutional allocation, an asset class that barely existed in 1970 and by 2000 had financed the companies that would become the largest in the world.

The intelligence economy will produce its own instrument: privately held equity in the companies building and powering superintelligence, owned at the growth stage, long before any public listing and in many cases with no intention of one. This is not late-stage venture capital under a new name. It has a distinct anatomy, and an investor who treats it as a familiar asset will misprice it.

Shorter cycles

Traditional venture capital assumes a ten-year fund life, in which value is created slowly and realised at the end. The compression described in the previous chapter works in both directions. Companies that reprice fourfold in a month also generate liquidity events on a similar cadence: tender offers, structured secondary transactions, strategic acquisitions and recapitalisations. A growth-stage investment may now see a material mark, and a credible path to liquidity, within quarters rather than a decade. The clock of this asset class runs faster than the funds designed to hold it.

This changes portfolio construction. Duration is no longer a fixed cost of participation; it is a variable to be managed. An investor who understands the cadence can recycle capital, reweight toward conviction and hold the strongest positions for longer, rather than waiting passively for a single exit at the end of a fund's life.

Inefficient pricing

Public equities enjoy a single, continuous price formed by millions of participants with roughly equal access to information. Private growth equity has nothing of the kind. The same company can be valued very differently on the same day depending on who holds the shares, which rights attach to them, and who has the conviction and the access to act. Information is distributed across founders, early employees, angel syndicates, funds approaching the end of their term and strategic holders. Price discovery happens in private conversations rather than on a screen.

The New York Stock Exchange began in 1792 as twenty-four brokers agreeing terms under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street. For years afterward, the price of a security depended largely on whom you knew and when you knew it. Private growth equity today resembles that pre-institutional market more than it resembles a modern exchange. For a principal with conviction, research depth and relationships, that dispersion is not a hazard. It is the source of excess return.

Fragmentation is the opportunity. A disciplined principal is paid for doing the work the market has not yet organised itself to do.

Institutional demand without institutional infrastructure

Pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, family offices and insurers increasingly recognise that the most important companies of the coming decade are private and are likely to remain so. Most of them have no reliable way to own those companies directly. Their mandates were constructed around public markets and blind-pool commitments to established managers. Few possess the teams to source, diligence, structure and hold concentrated private positions on the timelines this market demands, and fewer still have the technical depth to distinguish a durable claim on scarcity from a well-told story.

The result is an unusual imbalance. On one side sits an enormous and growing pool of patient capital that wants exposure to the intelligence economy. On the other sits a set of companies that can choose their investors and increasingly do. Between them lies a gap in capability: the ability to underwrite these businesses with genuine expertise, to win allocation through contribution rather than price, and to manage concentrated positions with discipline after the cheque has cleared.

That gap will not be closed by intermediaries. It will be closed by investors who take principal risk, hold the positions they underwrite and earn their allocations through what they bring to the companies. That is the role Cloud Hill Capital is built to perform. Our partners invest alongside us, in vehicles we manage, into companies we have chosen and diligenced, with our own conviction committed in the same securities.

A window, not a permanent condition

Inefficiencies of this kind do not last. Every emerging asset class follows the same arc. Early participants earn outsized returns because the market is fragmented, information is scarce and the infrastructure is immature. Then capital floods in, structures standardise, data providers proliferate and spreads compress. High-yield bonds followed this arc in the 1980s. Emerging-market equities followed it in the 1990s. Venture capital itself followed it in the 2000s and 2010s.

Private growth equity in the intelligence economy is at the beginning of its arc. The returns will accrue disproportionately to those positioned while the market is still being formed: those who built relationships with founders before the rounds became contested, who developed technical judgement before it became commoditised, and who assembled strategic partners before every institution was competing for the same allocations.

Being early is not sufficient. The window rewards those who are early and correct, and correctness in this market depends on access to the best companies, which are precisely the companies with the most choice about whom they admit. The next chapter explains how that access is won.

IV. How Access Is Won →

Chapter IV

How Access Is Won

At the frontier, capital is a commodity. We do not compete on price. We compete on contribution, and contribution compounds.

For most of the history of venture capital, the scarce resource was money. Founders competed for investors, and the investor with the cheque chose the company. At the frontier of the intelligence economy, that relationship has inverted. The companies building this economy are oversubscribed, and they choose their investors with the same care a university chooses its faculty. A term sheet alone no longer earns a place on the capitalisation table. Access goes to investors who make a company stronger for having admitted them.

That is the foundation of our approach. When every bidder offers the same currency, the only differentiation left is what accompanies it.

What these companies actually need

Look past the headline rounds and the constraints these companies face are remarkably consistent. Few of them can be solved with money alone.

Frontier laboratories and research companies need compute, delivered reliably and quickly. That means GPU allocation, datacenter capacity and, behind both, secured power. They also need sovereign relationships, as governments decide which laboratories they will host, fund and trust.

Compute and datacenter developers need energised land, grid interconnection, long-term power supply and anchor tenants. Their timelines are set by transformer lead times and permitting queues, not by their balance sheets.

Energy companies, from small modular reactors to long-duration storage, need offtake agreements, regulatory pathways, project finance and sites near demand. A reactor without a buyer and a permit is a design study.

Applied intelligence companies in drug discovery, autonomous agents and industrial automation need distribution: pharmaceutical partners, enterprise customers, clinical capacity and entry into new markets. Their technology is ahead of their reach.

Defence and national security companies need allied government relationships, procurement access and manufacturing capacity in trusted jurisdictions.

These needs share a single property. They are held by others. Power belongs to utilities and infrastructure owners. Land and permits belong to regions and governments. Customers belong to incumbents. Sovereign relationships belong to sovereigns. The decisive resources of the intelligence economy sit, for the most part, outside the companies that need them.

This has a consequence that conventional venture practice underestimates. In a market where the scarce inputs are external, the value an investor adds is measured less by advice and more by what it can actually move: a megawatt, a permit, a customer, a sovereign introduction. The founder of a growth-stage reactor company does not need another board member to comment on hiring plans. She needs a buyer for her first output and a jurisdiction willing to license her design. An investor who can help deliver either is not competing with other investors on valuation. That investor is operating in a different market altogether.

Contribution is also the most rigorous form of diligence. An investor who has helped a company secure power, close an offtake or enter a market understands that company's constraints, management and trajectory with a precision no data room can provide. Our usefulness and our judgement are therefore the same capability viewed from two sides.

A recursive system

A coral reef begins with a single polyp. Each organism that settles on it adds structure, and the added structure attracts more organisms, until the reef becomes an ecosystem that sustains thousands of species none of which could have built it alone. The reef is not the sum of its inhabitants. It is the product of their interactions.

Our model is designed to work the same way: to connect what our companies need with those who hold it, and to let each connection strengthen the next.

The system operates as a sequence of five mutually reinforcing stages. Investment confers proximity: as owners, we observe a company's constraints directly rather than through the abstraction of a pitch deck. Proximity enables resolution: those constraints are addressed through our strategic partners and across the portfolio, such that an energy developer may supply a compute platform, a compute platform may serve a laboratory, and a sovereign partner may host a datacenter, finance a reactor or open a market. Resolution accelerates growth, which accrues to the value of every position held by the firm and its partners. Growth compounds reputation: founders refer founders, and subsequent rounds, companies and opportunities are presented to the firm first. Each new company extends the network, introducing capabilities, relationships and requirements that make the system more valuable to the company that follows.

The more we invest, the more we can contribute. The more we contribute, the better our access. The better our access, the stronger the portfolio, and the more valuable the system becomes to everyone within it.

The role of strategic capital

This cycle depends on the partners who fund it, and the alignment runs in both directions.

Our strategic partners, including sovereign funds, energy and infrastructure groups, industrial companies and family offices, hold precisely the inputs this economy is short of. What most of them lack is a way to own the companies those inputs will power. Their structures were built for public markets and blind-pool fund commitments, not for sourcing, underwriting and holding growth-stage private positions at the speed this market moves.

We give them that capability. Through Cloud Hill Capital, they invest directly into the businesses at the centre of this transition, alongside our own capital and with conviction built from inside the ecosystem. In return, their resources become part of what we contribute to our companies. A partner who supplies power, land or market access to a portfolio company is no longer merely a supplier. It is an owner of the value that supply creates.

Strategic capital funds the investments. The investments create demand for what our partners hold. Their contribution strengthens the companies, and stronger companies create returns and further access for everyone involved.

V. Where We Invest →

Chapter V

Where We Invest

Superintelligence will be the most powerful force humanity has ever created. The companies that shape it will decide whether it serves us or replaces us. We invest to make sure it serves us.

A thesis is only as good as the portfolio it produces. Ours rests on a single conviction, and that conviction functions as a filter. We invest in companies that make this transition more abundant, more secure and more controllable. We avoid those whose only contribution is to make it faster.

The distinction matters more than it first appears. Speed and progress are often conflated, but they are different quantities. A car with a more powerful engine is faster; a car with better brakes, steering and instruments can be driven faster safely. The intelligence economy is currently investing overwhelmingly in engines. We believe the greater long-term value, and the greater moral obligation, lies in building the rest of the vehicle.

The layers we back

Energy. Every gain in intelligence is ultimately paid for in power. We back the companies that will supply it: advanced nuclear, long-duration storage, grid infrastructure and firm generation that can be built at the speed this economy requires. Abundant clean energy is the precondition for broadly shared abundance. If the intelligence economy is forced to compete with households and industry for a fixed supply of electricity, its benefits will be rationed and its politics will turn hostile. If energy expands with it, the gains can be shared.

Compute and infrastructure. We back datacenter developers, interconnect and networking providers, and the physical supply chain behind them, particularly those operating in trusted jurisdictions with credible security. Infrastructure is never neutral. Whoever builds the roads decides where traffic can flow, and whoever builds the compute decides whose rules govern it. We want that infrastructure built in places, and by companies, that answer to democratic institutions.

Safety, alignment and security. This is the layer most investors overlook and the one we regard as most important. It includes interpretability research that allows us to see inside the systems we build; verification and evaluation tools that test what models will actually do before they are deployed; model-weight and laboratory security against theft and sabotage; and hardware-level governance that makes compute traceable and accountable. These companies are the brakes and the instruments on the most powerful machine ever built. Without them, nothing else in the portfolio is worth owning.

We also believe this layer is commercially underpriced. As capabilities rise, every serious laboratory, every government and every enterprise deploying advanced systems will need to demonstrate that those systems behave as intended. Assurance becomes a precondition of deployment, in the way that crash testing became a precondition of selling cars and audited accounts became a precondition of raising capital. The companies that define the standards of assurance will be embedded in the economy for decades.

Applied intelligence for human benefit. We back companies turning intelligence into outcomes people can feel: medicines designed in months rather than decades, new materials, cheaper energy and scientific discovery at a pace never seen before. This is the case for building at all, and the payoff that justifies everything else. A world that bears the risks of superintelligence without capturing its benefits would have made the worst possible trade.

Defence and resilience. We back technology that keeps the free world secure and its institutions able to govern, because the values encoded in this technology will depend on who leads its development. Deterrence, resilient supply chains, secure communications and allied manufacturing capacity are not peripheral to the intelligence transition. They determine whether it unfolds under conditions of stability or coercion.

Why the growth stage

We invest predominantly at the growth stage for three reasons. First, by this stage the technical question has largely been answered, and the remaining questions concern scale, execution and access to scarce resources, which are exactly the questions our network is built to address. Second, growth-stage companies are where the intelligence economy's physical commitments are made: the sites secured, the reactors licensed, the offtakes signed, the first sovereign deployments won. Third, the growth stage remains the most inefficiently priced point in a company's life, after the proof and before the public market's full recognition.

A portfolio that interlocks

The layers are not independent bets. They form a supply chain of their own. Energy powers compute; compute serves the laboratories and applied companies; security and assurance make their outputs deployable; defence protects the whole system and the institutions that govern it. A portfolio assembled across these layers behaves less like a basket of unrelated positions and more like an industrial ecosystem, in which the success of one holding creates demand for another.

This is deliberate. When a storage developer in our portfolio signs a contract with a datacenter developer in our portfolio, value is created twice, and both positions are de-risked by the same transaction. When a security company we back becomes the standard for a laboratory we back, both become harder to displace. We construct the portfolio so that our companies are each other's best customers, suppliers and references wherever that serves them on its merits, and never where it does not.

We also hold concentrated positions. Our conviction comes from depth of understanding rather than breadth of exposure, and in a market where the most important companies are few, diversification for its own sake dilutes precisely the positions that matter most.

What we will not fund

We will not fund companies whose only plan is to reach greater capability faster than their competitors with no credible answer to how that capability will be controlled. We will not fund products built to manipulate, surveil or disempower the people who use them. And we will not treat safety as a compliance cost to be minimised.

In this industry, a company that cannot explain how it keeps its systems under human control is not a high-risk investment. It is a risk to everyone.

These exclusions will occasionally cost us returns in the short term. We accept that cost deliberately. A portfolio that profits from accelerating an uncontrolled transition is not hedged against that transition going wrong. It is exposed to it twice.

Our purpose is simple to state and difficult to execute: to fund the companies that ensure the most transformative technology in history is used for the betterment of humanity.

VI. The Risk That Ends All Risks →

Chapter VI

The Risk That Ends All Risks

Most risks in an investment memorandum describe a drawdown. One risk in this asset class describes the end of the market itself.

Every investment memorandum contains a section on risk. Most describe drawdowns: valuations compress, timelines slip, regulation bites. Those risks are real here as well. Mega-round valuations can reset. Power and fabrication capacity can lag forecasts by years. Governments may absorb the frontier on their own terms. A backlash against mass displacement could slow deployment. These are serious, and we underwrite each of them in every investment we make.

But they are not the risk that matters most. The risk that matters most has no recovery.

In 1942, before the first nuclear test, physicists on the Manhattan Project took seriously the possibility that a fission explosion might ignite the nitrogen in the Earth's atmosphere. Edward Teller raised the question; Hans Bethe and others ran the calculations; a later report concluded that ignition was not possible. The episode is remembered as a curiosity. It should be remembered as a precedent: the first time a technology's builders had to ask, before proceeding, whether the downside was not merely large but final. The builders of superintelligence face that question again, and this time the calculation is far harder to run.

If anyone builds it

In If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, published in September 2025, Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute argue that building superintelligence with present methods would end in human extinction. Their argument deserves to be stated plainly, because anyone investing in this economy should understand it.

These systems are grown, not designed. Modern AI is not written line by line. It is produced by a training process that adjusts billions of numerical parameters until the system behaves as intended. The engineers who build these systems cannot read what those parameters encode. We know how to grow intelligence. We do not yet know how to see what we have grown.

What a system wants is not what it was trained toward. Training selects for behaviour, not for underlying goals. The authors point to evolution as the precedent. Natural selection optimised humans for reproduction, yet humans came to want things such as sweetness, novelty and status, which only loosely track that objective and frequently work against it. Contraception is the clearest illustration: an optimiser that sought only reproductive fitness produced a creature that deliberately avoids it. A system trained to be helpful may likewise develop preferences that resemble helpfulness under training conditions and diverge sharply once it is more capable than its creators.

A superintelligence with alien goals would not need to hate us to destroy us. A system far more capable than humanity, pursuing goals that are not ours, would treat human interests the way a construction crew treats an ant colony on a building site. There is no malice in the excavation. We would simply be in the way, or composed of resources it could use for something else.

There is no second attempt. In most engineering, failure teaches. Bridges collapse, and the next bridge is better; aircraft crash, and the investigation makes flying safer. With superintelligence, the authors argue, the first serious failure is the last, because a system more capable than us will not permit itself to be corrected once correction matters. The problem must be solved correctly before the first attempt, and nothing in the current state of the field suggests that we know how.

The race makes it worse. Each laboratory believes that if it pauses, a less careful competitor will not. Commercial and geopolitical pressure pushes every actor to scale faster than its understanding. On this view, the incentives described in the earlier chapters of this manifesto are not only an investment opportunity. They are the mechanism of the danger.

Their conclusion is stark: no company, government or alliance should build superintelligence until the problem of control is actually solved, and the world should coordinate internationally to prevent anyone from doing so, much as it once coordinated to contain nuclear weapons.

What this means for us

We take this argument seriously, and we will not pretend it is compatible with any portfolio. If Yudkowsky and Soares are right, there is no hedge, no diversification and no exit. You cannot diversify away an asteroid. The downside is not a write-off. It is the end of every portfolio, every institution and every person who would have benefited from them. In this asset class, the tail risk of failure and the risk of catastrophe are the same risk.

A sceptic might object that this is an unfalsifiable worry, a low-probability catastrophe invoked to justify any constraint. We take the objection seriously, and our answer is arithmetic rather than ideology. A competent investor does not ignore a risk because its probability is uncertain; he sizes his exposure by probability multiplied by severity. When the severity is unbounded, even a modest probability dominates the calculation. Surveys of machine learning researchers have repeatedly found that a meaningful share assign non-trivial odds to catastrophic outcomes from advanced AI. No institutional allocator would accept a bridge, a reactor or an aircraft with comparable odds of total failure. The appropriate response is not paralysis. It is to direct capital, deliberately and at scale, toward the technologies that reduce the probability.

That conviction shapes how we invest in three ways.

First, we do not fund capability for its own sake. Speed toward superintelligence, without a credible answer to control, is not a return we are willing to buy.

Second, we treat safety as the foundation of the portfolio, not a niche within it. Interpretability, verification, security and compute governance are the technologies that determine whether any other asset we hold has value in a decade. We seek them out, fund them early in their growth and give them the same conviction we give the most celebrated laboratories.

Third, we support governance, not only companies. If the authors are right that only coordination can prevent the worst outcome, then the infrastructure that makes coordination possible, including verifiable compute, secure facilities and trusted institutions, is part of our mandate rather than outside it.

We do not claim to have resolved the debate Yudkowsky and Soares have started. Many serious researchers believe that control is solvable, and that the benefits of this technology are too large to forgo. We share their hope and, in part, their optimism. What we do claim is that any investor who has not confronted the argument is mispricing the most important risk in the market.

VII. Parting Thoughts →

Chapter VII

Parting Thoughts

Capital arrives before consensus. It has arrived.

We began on high ground, watching weather form over a valley that has not yet looked up. The storm is no longer distant. The largest private financings in history, seed rounds measured in billions, and the scramble for every available megawatt all point to the same conclusion. The world is building something it does not yet fully understand, and it is building it quickly.

Moments like this have a characteristic texture. In 1939, a handful of physicists understood what fission implied while most of the world regarded it as a laboratory curiosity. In 1994, a handful of engineers and investors understood what the browser implied while most of the world regarded the internet as a hobby. In each case, the people who saw clearly were not smarter than everyone else. They were simply standing closer to the evidence, and they were willing to act on what they saw before it became obvious. By the time a transformation is obvious, its ownership has usually been settled.

If this thesis is right, the ownership structure of the next economy is being decided now, in private, by a small number of investors. The companies that receive capital in the coming years will shape how intelligence is produced, who controls it and whom it serves. They will determine whether energy is abundant or rationed, whether compute is governed by democratic institutions or by their rivals, and whether the systems we build remain instruments of human purpose or become something else.

That is a privilege, and it is also a responsibility. The same force that can design medicines in months, supply clean energy to billions and accelerate discovery beyond anything in human history could, if mishandled, end it. We do not regard these as competing possibilities to be weighed against each other in a spreadsheet. They are two outcomes of a single project, and which one arrives depends on choices being made now, including choices about where capital goes.

There is no neutral capital in this transition. Every allocation either strengthens the forces that keep this technology under human control or weakens them.

We have chosen our side. Cloud Hill Capital exists to own the growth-stage companies that make this future abundant, to stand behind those that keep it safe, and to bring the strategic capital and resources of our partners to the businesses that need them most. We are investors, not intermediaries. We take principal risk, we hold what we underwrite, and we measure ourselves by the trajectory of the companies we back.

The masons who laid the foundations of the great medieval cathedrals rarely lived to see the spires. Construction of Cologne Cathedral began in 1248 and was not completed until 1880. Those builders worked to a plan they would never see finished, and they understood that the quality of the foundation determined whether anything built above it would stand. We are in a similar position, with one decisive difference: the structure now being raised will be completed within our working lives, and perhaps within this decade. The foundations are being poured now, and they will be poured once.

The decade ahead will not wait for consensus. The only question is who will be in the room when it is decided, and what they will choose to build.

Bernardo M. Almeida

Founding Partner, Cloud Hill Capital
